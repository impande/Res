Hobbies in Resume: Best Hobbies for Freshers (With Examples)

Hobbies can make a fresher resume more memorable — if you pick the right ones. Here are the 25 best hobbies for freshers (and what each one tells an employer), 50+ more ideas, the ones to avoid, and exactly how to write them.

Do hobbies actually help on a resume?

Yes — in the right situation. If you're a fresher or student with limited work experience, a few well-chosen hobbies show personality, soft skills and culture fit, and help fill a short resume. If you're experienced, hobbies are optional and should never push out real achievements. The key is to be specific: "captain of the college football team" says far more than "sports".

What makes a hobby worth listing

  • It hints at a useful trait — teamwork, discipline, leadership, creativity, curiosity.
  • It's specific — "writing a tech blog with 2k readers" beats "writing".
  • It's positive and safe — nothing political, religious or controversial.
  • It's genuine — be ready to talk about it in an interview.

25 best hobbies for resume for freshers (and what each shows employers)

As a fresher, your hobbies section is one of the few places a recruiter learns who you are outside marks and projects. These 25 hobbies work well because each one points to a skill employers actually hire for. Pick 3–5 that are genuinely yours and that suit the job.

Sports & fitness

  • Team sports — cricket, football, kabaddi, volleyballShows: teamwork, handling pressure and turning up for practice.
  • Running or cyclingShows: self-discipline and setting and tracking personal goals.
  • Yoga or gym fitnessShows: consistency, stress management and a healthy routine.
  • Trekking and hikingShows: planning, resilience and comfort with the unfamiliar.

Thinking & strategy

  • ChessShows: strategic thinking, patience and planning several steps ahead.
  • Puzzles, Sudoku or Rubik’s cubeShows: logical problem-solving and focus.
  • Reading (name the genre — business, biographies, sci-fi)Shows: curiosity, vocabulary and the ability to concentrate.
  • Learning a new languageShows: dedication and communication — useful for customer-facing and MNC roles.
  • QuizzingShows: broad general knowledge and quick recall under time pressure.

Creative

  • PhotographyShows: an eye for detail and composition.
  • Sketching or paintingShows: creativity and patience to finish what you start.
  • Playing a musical instrumentShows: long-term discipline and regular practice.
  • Blogging or creative writingShows: clear written communication and consistency.
  • Video editing and content creationShows: digital skills and storytelling for an audience.
  • Graphic design (Canva, Figma)Shows: visual communication and familiarity with design tools.

Tech & self-learning

  • Coding side projectsShows: self-learning and practical skills beyond the syllabus.
  • DIY electronics or robotics (Arduino, Raspberry Pi)Shows: hands-on problem-solving and curiosity about how things work.
  • Online courses (NPTEL, Coursera, YouTube tutorials)Shows: initiative to keep learning without being told to.
  • Following the stock market or personal financeShows: analytical thinking and financial awareness.

People & community

  • Volunteering (NSS, NCC, an NGO)Shows: responsibility, empathy and reliability.
  • Teaching or tutoring juniorsShows: patience and the ability to explain things clearly.
  • Public speaking or debatingShows: confidence and persuasive communication.
  • Organising or anchoring college eventsShows: leadership, coordination and working to deadlines.
  • Cooking or bakingShows: planning, following a process and creativity.
  • Planning budget trips with friendsShows: organisation, budgeting and adaptability.

Tip: add one concrete detail to your strongest hobby — a level, a duration, a result or a link. "Chess" is fine; "Chess — 2nd place, inter-college tournament" is memorable.

Hobbies to avoid

  • Vague or passive: "watching TV", "listening to music", "sleeping", "surfing the internet".
  • Controversial: politics, religion, anything divisive.
  • Anything that could worry a recruiter about safety or focus.
  • Scrolling social media or binge-watching series — reads as time-pass, not a skill.
  • Betting, gambling or money-based fantasy games — raises questions about judgement.
  • Partying or clubbing — fine in life, but adds nothing on a resume.
  • "Gaming" on its own — say something specific instead, e.g. "Captain of the college esports team" or "Building small games in Unity", if it's relevant.
  • Anything you can't talk about for two minutes — interviewers often ask, and a made-up hobby is easy to spot.

How to write the hobbies line in a resume (format examples)

Use the heading "Hobbies & Interests" (or just "Hobbies" or "Interests"), place it near the bottom — after skills, certifications and achievements, and before the declaration if you use one — and list 3–5 items. Here are three formats that work:

Format 1 — one line (most common)
Captain, college football team · Tech blogging (2k monthly readers) · Chess · Volunteering with a local NGO
Format 2 — short bullets with a detail
• Chess — 2nd place, inter-college tournament 2025
• Photography — run an Instagram page on Nagpur street food
• Teaching — tutor two Class 8 students in maths on weekends
Format 3 — interests + hobbies (good for MBA & commerce)
Interests: personal finance, start-up news, behavioural economics
Hobbies: badminton, reading business biographies, cooking
Weak
Hobbies: Reading, music, travelling, internet surfing, cricket
Better
Hobbies & Interests: Reading business biographies · Playing the keyboard (6 years) · Trekking in the Western Ghats · Cricket — college team opener

Keep it to one line or a small section near the bottom. A few sharp, specific hobbies beat a long generic list. Don't use icons or images for hobbies — ATS software can't read them, so type them as plain text.

Frequently asked questions

Should I put hobbies on my resume?

Only if they add value — which they often do for freshers and students with limited experience. A few relevant, specific hobbies can show personality, soft skills and culture fit. If you're experienced and space is tight, skip them in favour of achievements.

What are good hobbies to put on a resume?

Specific, positive ones that hint at useful traits: reading, blogging, chess, coding side-projects, playing a team sport, volunteering, photography, public speaking or learning a language. Avoid vague ('watching TV') or risky ones (politics, religion).

How many hobbies should I list?

Three to five, in a short line or small section. Keep it brief — hobbies support your resume, they don't lead it.

Where do hobbies go on a resume?

In a small 'Hobbies & Interests' section near the bottom, after education, skills and experience. For freshers it can help fill a short resume; for experienced candidates it's optional.

What hobbies should I avoid on a resume?

Avoid anything controversial (politics, religion), passive or generic ('sleeping', 'watching movies'), or anything that could raise concerns about focus. Keep it professional and positive.

What are the best hobbies for a fresher resume?

The best hobbies for freshers are ones that hint at a skill employers want and that you can talk about in an interview: a team sport (teamwork), chess (strategic thinking), reading or blogging (communication), coding side projects (self-learning), volunteering with NSS or an NGO (responsibility), photography or design (creativity) and public speaking or debating (confidence). Pick 3–5 that are true for you and suit the job.

What is the correct format for hobbies in a resume?

Use a heading such as 'Hobbies & Interests' near the bottom of the resume, after skills and certifications. List 3–5 hobbies on one line separated by commas or dots, or as short bullets, and add a specific detail to one or two — for example 'Chess (district-level player)' or 'Photography (Instagram page on street food)'.

Is 'listening to music' or 'travelling' a good hobby for a resume?

On their own they are too common to say anything about you. Make them specific and active: 'Playing the tabla (trained for 6 years)' instead of 'listening to music', or 'Planning budget trips and trekking in the Sahyadris' instead of 'travelling'. If you can't add a detail, choose a different hobby.

Should hobbies be related to the job I'm applying for?

They don't have to be, but at least one related hobby helps. A coding side project supports an IT application, following the stock market supports a commerce or finance role, and tutoring supports a teaching job. The rest can simply show you are disciplined, sociable or curious.

Do interviewers ask about hobbies on a resume?

Yes, especially in fresher interviews, where 'Tell me about your hobbies' is a common warm-up question. Only list hobbies you genuinely do, and be ready to give one example of what you did and what it taught you. Practise your answer with our free interview prep tool.
